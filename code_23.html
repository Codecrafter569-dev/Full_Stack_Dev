Ravi moved into the apartment on the twelfth floor because it was cheap — suspiciously cheap — and the landlord had only one condition: don't answer the door between 3 and 4 in the morning, it's an old building, it makes sounds. The first week was fine. Then, on the eighth night, a knock came at 3:14 a.m. — three slow knocks, followed by a voice, soft and familiar, his mother's: "Ravi? Beta, open the door, it's cold out here." Ravi sat frozen, because his mother lived in Nagpur, and when he called her the next morning she said she'd been asleep all night. The knocks returned every night after that, always around 3 a.m., always in a familiar voice — his mother, his best friend, once even his own voice calling from outside while he sat inside listening. He learned to live with it. He stopped hearing it. Then one night, weeks later, the voice came from inside the apartment — from the hallway, just outside his bedroom door — and it said, "Ravi? Why haven't you been answering?" And from behind the front door, faint and desperate, came his own voice, knocking to be let in.
